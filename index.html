The problem

Some time ago I walked next to one of the wetlands in Bogotá. I expected to see birds and plants, and I saw them. But I also saw plastic bottles and bags in the water. I had seen photos like this on the news many times, but I had never seen it so close. That is how this project started for me.

Plastic pollution is a big problem in my city. If it rains, the water carries plastic from the streets to the canals, and the canals take it to the wetlands and the Bogotá River. Plastic does not disappear. If it stays in the water, it breaks into microplastics that hurt animals and, in the end, people.

The amount of waste is also growing. In 2024 Bogotá produced 2.29 million tons of waste, and in 2025 it produced 2.42 million tons. Most of it goes to the Doña Juana landfill, and only a small part is recycled.

2

Canada and Colombia

In Phase 1 I studied how Canada fights plastic pollution. When I compared it with Colombia, I found more similarities than I expected. Both countries have laws, and both still have a lot of plastic in landfills and in nature.

TopicCanadaColombia
RecyclingOnly 9% of plastic was recycled in 2016. More than 3.2 million tonnes became garbage.In Bogotá, only a small part of the waste is recovered. Waste pickers (recicladores) do most of this work.
LawsThe Single-use Plastics Prohibition Regulations banned six items: bags, cutlery, food containers, ring carriers, stir sticks and straws.Law 2232 of 2022 banned eight single-use products from July 2024 (bags, straws, stirrers and others). More products will be banned in 2030.
Prices and habitsThe ban stops the problem at the source: stores cannot sell these items.Since July 2017 people pay a small tax for each plastic bag at the store.
Separating wasteEach city organizes its own recycling program.Since January 2021 there is one national color code: white for recyclables, black for non-recyclables, green for organic waste.
Community actionThe Great Canadian Shoreline Cleanup, with more than one million volunteers since 1994.Local cleanups exist, but they are smaller and less known.

The biggest difference I found is not the laws. It is the community. In Canada, ordinary people have cleaned shorelines every year for three decades. In Bogotá, most people still think that the government or the waste pickers will solve the problem.

3

My solutions for Bogotá

In Phase 2 I designed an action plan with five ideas from Canada, adapted to Bogotá. If students participate, the campaign will be more successful. And if I were the mayor, I would put recycling points in every neighborhood.

From the Shoreline Cleanup · the center of my campaign

The Great Bogotá Cleanup

One Sunday every month, volunteers clean a wetland or the banks of the Bogotá River and count the plastic they collect.

From Ocean Legacy

Plastic Back to Life

Collection points in universities and community centers. The clean plastic goes to waste picker organizations.

From Litterless Lunches

Litterless Lunch Challenge

For one week, students bring their lunch in reusable containers. The group with the least plastic wins.

From Canadian cafés

Bring Your Own Cup

Partner cafés give a 10% discount to people who bring their own cup, so we use fewer disposable cups.

From education campaigns

Learn and Separate

Short videos and school talks to explain the white, black and green bags.

What had happened before?

CanadaBefore the ban, Canada had produced a lot of plastic waste for many years. In 2016 alone, more than 3.2 million tonnes became garbage. Only 9% of the plastic had been recycled, and most of it had gone to landfills.

ColombiaBefore 2017, stores had given plastic bags for free for many years, and people had never thought about how many they used. Before 2021, each city had used its own colors to separate waste.

What had people done?

CanadaBefore the cleanup became national in 2002, volunteers had cleaned beaches in British Columbia for eight years. By 1997, 400 volunteers had joined at 20 places. Since 1994, more than one million volunteers have registered.

ColombiaFor decades, waste pickers had collected recyclable materials from the streets, often without recognition. Many people had not realized that their work was a public service.

What had governments achieved?

CanadaBy December 2022, the government had banned making and importing six single-use plastic items. By the end of 2023, stores had stopped selling most of them.

ColombiaBy the end of 2018, the bag tax had reduced bag use by 53%. When the first bans started in July 2024, Congress had already approved Law 2232 two years before.

What lessons had they learned?

CanadaCanada had learned that recycling alone was not enough, because very little plastic had been recycled. So they decided to stop some plastics at the source.

ColombiaColombia had learned that a small price can change a habit. By 2018, many people had started to bring their own bags.

What can Colombia learn from Canada?

Laws are important, but they are not enough. Canada's biggest cleanup did not start with the government. It started with a few people and one beach, and it grew because they did it every year. Bogotá can do the same with its wetlands: start small, do it every month, and share the results so more people join.

What I learned

Before this project, I had never heard about the Great Canadian Shoreline Cleanup. What surprised me most was how it started. I had imagined a big government program, but it had begun with a few aquarium workers and volunteers on one beach. By the time I read about it, more than one million people had registered as volunteers.

I had always thought that big changes need a lot of money or a new law. This story showed me that a small group can start something big if they are patient. That is why the monthly cleanup is the center of my campaign.
